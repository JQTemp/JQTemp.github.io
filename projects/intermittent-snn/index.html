Intermittent spiking neural networks for wearable EMG

A framework that lets a spiking neural network keep its progress through power loss on a battery-free wearable, by saving only the state it needs. I developed it in my master's thesis and extended it as a research assistant into a first-author paper at IEEE EMBC, with hand gesture recognition from forearm EMG as the test case.

The idea

AI makes wearables more capable but also more power hungry, which limits how long they run on a battery. Two ideas attack this from different sides. Spiking neural networks only compute when spikes arrive, which makes them far more efficient than conventional networks on suitable hardware. Intermittent computing lets a device run from harvested energy, such as solar or thermal energy, by saving its state to non-volatile memory before power runs out and resuming once enough energy is back. To our knowledge, no one had combined the two before.

What a checkpoint has to hold

A checkpoint only needs what changes during inference. The weights and the input encoding stay fixed, so they never need saving. What does need saving depends on the neuron model, the output decoding and where in the network power is lost. Leaky integrate-and-fire neurons only carry a membrane potential, while second-order neurons also carry a synaptic current. With rate decoding every output spike so far has to be kept, while with latency decoding the first output spike ends the inference.

Position matters too. Checkpoints in the first and final timesteps are smaller than those in between, because part of the network state does not exist yet or is no longer needed, and after the last layer of the final timestep nothing needs saving at all.

When a checkpoint pays off

Writing a checkpoint costs energy and time, so saving after every layer is wasteful. The framework places a checkpoint only once the energy spent since the previous one exceeds the energy needed to write a new one, so a checkpoint never costs more than the work it protects. It also rules out positions where the checkpoint plus the rest of the inference would cost more than starting over. For the EMG network this leaves seven checkpoints out of forty possible positions (Figure 1).

Test case, gestures from forearm EMG

To test the framework I built a convolutional spiking network in snnTorch for the Ninapro DB2 dataset, in which 12 electrodes on the forearm record surface EMG while people perform hand and wrist movements. The network takes windows from all 12 electrodes, turns them into spike trains with rate encoding and runs them through one convolution layer and three fully connected layers of second-order leaky integrate-and-fire neurons over ten timesteps. I tuned the layer sizes and neuron parameters with Optuna on DTU's GPU cluster.

Trained on one subject's recordings of the first 12 gestures of exercise B, the network reaches about 70% test accuracy, in line with comparable spiking models. The aim was not a record accuracy but a realistic network to study intermittent operation on.

Results

Energy is estimated analytically from memory accesses and synaptic operations, following Lemaire et al., and the cost of each checkpoint comes from the datasheet of a non-volatile FRAM memory. Without checkpoints one inference costs about 503 µJ, most of it in the convolution layer. The checkpoints placed by the framework raise that to 884 µJ, while a checkpoint after every layer would cost 3,479 µJ, almost seven times as much (Figure 2).

The price is latency, since writing the checkpoints adds about 350 ms per inference. That is acceptable for monitoring that can tolerate short gaps, but not for tasks that need an instant response.

All energy and latency figures are estimates. The project was accepted into Intel's Neuromorphic Research Community, which would have given access to the Loihi chip, but the agreement could not be finalised in time, so everything ran in simulation. Measuring the framework on real neuromorphic hardware is the natural next step.

From thesis to paper

The master's thesis, carried out in the Embedded Systems Engineering group at DTU Compute, laid the groundwork with a three-phase feasibility study covering what a checkpoint in a spiking network must contain, the trade-offs against an uninterrupted network and a strategy for placing checkpoints. Over six months as a research assistant I then retuned the network, which raised test accuracy from about 61% to 70%, refined the evaluation and wrote the paper with my co-authors.

The thesis code, including the checkpointing notebook, is on GitHub.
